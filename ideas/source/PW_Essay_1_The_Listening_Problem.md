## Thesis

For almost all of human history, music was something you did, not something you consumed. The technologies of the 20th century — recorded sound, the radio, the personal stereo, streaming — transformed music into a product delivered to a passive recipient. This was not a neutral shift. It changed what music does to us and for us, by severing the listening experience from the participation that was always its most important dimension. Extended reality (XR) technology creates, for the first time, a practical path back to participatory music — not by abandoning the abundance that recording gave us, but by restoring the layer it took away.

---

## I. What Music Was Before It Became a Product

Before the phonograph, there was no such thing as a recording. There was no such thing as a music industry in any recognizable sense. What existed was music as a practice — something communities did together, regularly, as a constitutive part of social life.

The sociologist Christopher Small captured this with the concept of 'musicking' — treating music as a verb rather than a noun. Musicking encompasses all the roles in a musical event: performing, listening, practicing, dancing, even setting up the space. By Small's account, music is not primarily a product to be consumed but a social activity to be participated in, and its meaning lies in the web of relationships the activity creates — between performers, between performers and listeners, between everyone present and the wider world the event temporarily brings into being. This was not only true of formal performance. Work songs accompanied collective labor. Communal singing happened around tables, in fields, in places of worship, at social gatherings. Children learned music by participation before instruction. The overwhelming norm was that if music was happening, the people present were making it — or at a minimum, actively engaged with it rather than passively receiving it from a speaker.

Professional performance existed, but it was the exception rather than the rule, accessible to few and experienced infrequently. The average person's relationship to music was primarily one of making and participating, not one of listening to others perform.

This matters because the physiological effects of musical participation are fundamentally different from those of passive listening. When people make music together — sing, play, move in coordinated response to shared rhythm — measurable neurological and biochemical events occur: brains entrain their oscillatory activity to each other (inter-brain synchrony), oxytocin levels rise, endorphins elevate, respiratory and heart rhythms synchronize between participants. These are the physiological substrates of social bonding. They are triggered by collective participation. They are not reliably triggered by listening alone.

## II. What Recording Did — and What It Cost

The invention of recorded sound in the late 19th century — the phonograph (1877), the radio (1920s), the LP (1948), the personal stereo (1979), the MP3 and streaming platforms — was one of the most consequential technological transitions in cultural history. Its benefits are real and significant: music became available to anyone, anywhere, at any time; musical traditions were preserved that would otherwise have been lost; recorded music created genuinely new forms of cross-cultural connection.

But the economic model that the recording industry required — music produced once by professionals, packaged as a commodity, and consumed by passive audiences — inverted a ratio that had held for all of human history. For hundreds of thousands of years, the overwhelming majority of musical experience was participatory. In approximately four generations, that ratio was reversed. Today, the overwhelming majority of musical experience is passive listening — often alone.

The costs of this inversion have not been adequately accounted for.

Passive listening to recorded music is not without value. The experience of hearing music that speaks to something true in your emotional life is a real form of contact — the musician has expressed something real, and through the recording they communicate across time and space that you are not entirely alone in what you feel. This matters. But it is a thin substitute for the real thing, and in a specific way: the musician is not present. You are not making anything together. The sound travels in one direction only.

The social bonding effects that participatory music activates are simply not available through passive listening. No amount of time spent alone with a playlist produces the inter-brain synchrony, the shared oxytocin release, the cardiovascular and respiratory entrainment that happen when people make music together. Streaming platforms deliver music. They do not deliver music's most important social function.

This is not a small loss. Music's social function — its capacity to create the felt physiological reality of collective belonging — is among the most ancient and important things music does. It preceded recorded music by orders of magnitude. It was the basis on which every durable human cultural tradition was built. The participatory musical ritual is what makes doctrine feel true in the body rather than merely true in the mind; what produces courage in people facing danger together; what maintains cultural coherence across generations and geographies. The recording did not replace this. It occluded it — by making the reduced version so abundantly available that the need driving the demand was partially satisfied without the deeper need ever being met.

## III. The Listening Trap

There is a paradox in how passive consumption of recorded music relates to the social need it partially serves. It functions simultaneously as remedy and as a perpetuating cause of the condition it treats.

Listening to music alone is a remedy for loneliness. It provides a simulated form of the belonging that collective musical participation produces — the sense that another human has felt what you are feeling, that somewhere a community of people know this music and are therefore, in some latent sense, your people. For someone without access to participatory musical life, the record is not nothing. It is real, if reduced, contact.

But because the remedy works well enough — because passive listening delivers enough of the feeling to relieve the acute pressure of isolation — it reduces the motivation to seek the full experience. A person who can satisfy the hunger for musical belonging by putting on headphones is less likely to seek out a choir, a jam session, a communal music-making context. The relief of the symptom forestalls the treatment of the cause.

The recording industry did not intend this outcome. But the structural logic of a palliative — something that manages the symptom while the underlying deficit deepens — describes what passive consumption of recorded music does to the need for musical participation. Music is everywhere, more accessible than at any point in history, and people are measurably more isolated.

## IV. The Missing Middle

The recording industry's professionalization of music created a binary that had not previously existed at this scale: trained musicians who produce, passive audiences who consume. Most musical cultures throughout history have had a wide middle ground between these poles — people who were neither professional performers nor passive listeners, but active participants. They sang at communal gatherings, played informally at home, kept rhythm in ritual, danced in ways that shaped the music rather than merely responding to it. This middle layer was not a lesser form of musical life. It was, for most people in most cultures for most of history, the primary form.

Recording and its commercial imperatives gradually contracted this middle ground. When music became a product made by specialists and delivered to consumers, the role of the non-specialist shrank toward audience. The threshold for participation rose: if music sounds like a recording, and you don't sound like a recording, the implicit message is that your contribution is not the right kind.

XR music environments can restore this middle layer by making meaningful musical participation accessible below the threshold of professional production skill. Not passive listening, and not full DAW-based production — but something in between: gesture-based instruments, accessible patching systems, collaborative environments where contribution of any kind shapes the musical result. PatchWorld operates in this space while also supporting deeper creator workflows for advanced users. The range of entry points is, in itself, part of the argument.

## V. What XR Changes

Extended reality technologies — particularly social VR platforms — do not resolve this paradox automatically. A VR concert where users passively watch an avatar perform behind a stage replicates the problem in a new medium. Spectator VR is not the answer.

What XR uniquely enables is something different: genuine participatory musical experience between people who are physically separated by any distance. For the first time in history, the barrier to communal music-making is not proximity. You do not need to be in the same room as someone to make music with them in a way that activates the same physiological and social processes that making music in the same room does.

This is a meaningful development, not a marketing claim. The mechanisms of musical participation that produce social bonding — the synchronization of movement, breath, and attention; the shared acoustic space in which voices and instruments merge — these require shared musical activity, not shared physical space. Two people improvising together in a shared virtual environment, hearing each other in real time, responding to each other's musical contributions, are doing the thing that produces the effects. The fact that their bodies are geographically separated does not change the nature of the musical interaction.

For collaboration specifically, XR removes the most significant practical barrier to participatory music-making: the requirement that participants be in the same physical location. Historically, musical collaboration required either co-location or the slow, asynchronous exchange of recordings. Real-time musical collaboration at a distance has been technically possible through internet audio for some years, but it has never been *embodied* in the way that matters — the participants have been sitting alone at desks, interacting through mouse and keyboard, rather than inhabiting a shared sonic and visual space together.

Social VR platforms capable of hosting musical collaboration — where participants can build instruments, patch together audio and visual systems, perform for each other, and engage as present bodies in a shared space rather than as distant signals in a chat window — represent the first practical technology for participatory musical experience at global scale.

PatchWorld is a current example of this. Users on opposite sides of the world can enter the same virtual environment, build and play instruments together in real time, perform for each other and for audiences, and experience the collaboration as an embodied activity — reaching, connecting, responding — rather than as an asynchronous file transfer. The social VR layer does not merely add a communication channel. It restores the shared space that communal music-making has always required.

This does not make physical co-presence unnecessary or less valuable. The argument is not that virtual musical collaboration is equivalent to or better than physical musical collaboration. The argument is that it is vastly better than no musical collaboration — that for the billions of people for whom the physical alternative is genuinely unavailable (due to geography, economics, time, health, or social circumstance), XR musical participation is a real and meaningful restoration of something that passive consumption of recorded music cannot provide.

## VI. The Collaboration Dividend

Beyond the social dimension, XR musical collaboration enables something with no direct historical precedent: participation in musical creation by people without traditional musical training, through interfaces that make musical contribution accessible regardless of technical skill.

One of the significant barriers to participatory music-making in contemporary life is the perception (and to some extent the reality) that musical participation requires skills that take years to develop. This was not always the case — the communal singing traditions that were standard in earlier centuries required no more skill than learning the words and the melody, and many participatory music traditions across world cultures have always been accessible to untrained participants.

The recording industry's professionalization of music — the separation of skilled producers from passive consumers — has reinforced the perception that music-making is a specialist activity. This is a historically anomalous view. The specialization is real but relatively recent and by no means universal.

XR music environments can bridge this gap by offering interfaces that make musical contribution genuinely accessible: sequencers that quantize timing automatically while preserving expressiveness, instruments that respond to gesture and movement in musically meaningful ways without requiring precise technical execution, and visual and spatial interfaces that make the structure of musical systems immediately comprehensible rather than requiring years of theoretical study.

This does not mean removing all barriers or flattening all skill distinctions. Expert musicians in XR environments can develop complex, demanding practices. But the same environment can provide accessible entry points for people who have never considered themselves musicians, creating the conditions for genuine musical participation across a much wider population than the recording industry's professional-consumer divide allows.

## Conclusion: Not a Return — An Advance

The argument here is not that the recording industry was a mistake, or that streaming should be replaced by mandated communal singing. Recorded music has produced genuine goods that should not be abandoned. The cross-cultural musical exchange enabled by recording — the global propagation of forms and traditions that has created new kinds of human connection across geographic and demographic boundaries — is real and valuable.

The argument is that recorded music, for all its abundance, solved a distribution problem while creating a participation deficit. It made music available everywhere while making participation the exception rather than the norm. XR technology — specifically social XR platforms capable of hosting real-time musical collaboration — creates the first practical path toward restoring participation at scale, without requiring either the abandonment of recorded music's benefits or physical co-presence as a prerequisite for meaningful musical experience together.

The question worth asking is not whether this technology is ready. For basic musical collaboration, it already is. The question is whether the cultural understanding of what music is for — not primarily entertainment, not primarily a commodity, but primarily a social technology for producing the physiological reality of collective belonging — is adequate to the opportunity that the technology is creating.
